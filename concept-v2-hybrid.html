Burst

Burst came out of a frustration I kept hitting: getting from an idea to a running experiment shouldn't start with a day of infrastructure setup. So now you send a long-running job from your laptop, it starts on an isolated microVM in about 90 to 120 milliseconds, runs to completion, and the results come back, with nothing to set up or manage. The first customer runs a drug-discovery pipeline.

Makerspace

I co-founded a makerspace as a student, and after Microsoft Research I came back to co-lead it for four years. It began in a couple of retrofitted classrooms and grew into a 4,500 square foot shop, with more than 2,400 members over the years and around forty student managers keeping it running. A lot of them were studying philosophy or history long before they touched a 3D printer, and a first-year teaching a PhD student how to drive the CNC was an ordinary afternoon. Work from the space has since reached HCI International 2025 and ACM Compute 2025.

03

CovidBloc

As part of a blockchain research fellowship with Dr. Mahavir Jhawar, a friend and I built CovidBloc during the lockdowns, a contact-tracing system that keeps its exposure database on a private Hyperledger Fabric network, following the Apple and Google exposure-notification design. They wrote the Flutter app; I built the backend and ran the chain itself, a permissioned Hyperledger Fabric network I set up across several GCP Compute Engine instances, from the certificate authority and MSP through the peer and the full chaincode lifecycle. We put it in front of about fifteen people on a mostly empty campus. It was the first distributed system of this kind I had stood up and run end to end, and it taught me more about infrastructure than any class had. The writeup and a demo live on Dr. Jhawar's research group page.

04

Balancing act: the red fox of the Trans-Himalaya

I wrote the software for eight motion-triggered acoustic units, each a Raspberry Pi with a custom HAT inside a weatherproof enclosure my colleagues built, for a team at the National Centre for Biological Sciences. They went out to Spiti Valley and sat at 5,000 metres through a winter that dropped to thirty below, often under six feet of snow. No one could reach them for days at a time, so the software had to keep recording cleanly on its own between battery swaps and not lock up. Those recordings became a paper in Proceedings of the Royal Society B about how red foxes manage to live alongside the dogs that hunt them, and I am one of the authors.

05

AGON

AGON started as a question for a theory of computation course: how much smaller could the same data get if you stopped making it human-readable? Adhiraj Singh and I answered it with a compact binary format built on a small formal grammar, where every value carries a one-byte type marker, so a parser reads it in a single pass with no lookahead. Across 225 generated objects, about half of them deeply nested, it encoded around 70% faster than JSON and produced files around 70% smaller. Decoding came out slower than JSON, which is the obvious thing to fix next.

06

The Indian Car Attack

This one started in my dorm room, with no hardware lab. I sourced a key fob and central-locking unit from a dealer and went after the radio with an RTL-SDR and a YARD Stick One. A plain replay failed, because the fob uses rolling codes, so I worked through a roll-jam and a one-shot replay-at-a-distance instead, and traced the fob to a Microchip HCS200 running Classic KeeLoq, a cipher broken publicly since 2008. With physical access to the locking unit, the attack unlocked it. The project later grew into a small car-hacking group inside the makerspace.

07

Palynology microscope automation

With Dr. Meghna Agarwala and Ananya Dinkar, I have been building low-cost microscopy automation for palynology, the study of pollen. Identifying a fossil grain means tumbling it to see it from several angles, something palynologists still do by hand, one tap at a time. We automate that with a roughly $300 servo rig driven by an ESP32, paired with open-source focus-stacking and slide-scanning software that captures a few hundred frames per sample. Early testing points to taking an analysis that runs to about a year down toward roughly three months. We are building it out with groups at the Smithsonian and Oxford, and it is headed to a technical workshop at INQUA 2027.
